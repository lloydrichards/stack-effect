Choose the exploration story.
Then choose the graph.

Seven visual references for deciding how people should discover modules, understand dependencies, and explore across catalogs. Each reference pairs a real example with a possible role in Stack Effect.

Working hypothesis: an overview and a focused explanation may deserve different views.

A force map can reveal neighborhoods. A dependency diagram can explain a consequence. A matrix can compare many relationships. Our first decision is which question the explorer should answer best.

What should someone leave understanding?

01 / Obsidian

“What is around this thing?”

Observed pattern
Global and local graphs, connection highlighting, configurable groups, and adjustable local depth.
Catalog adaptation
Start from a target kind or a module; reveal a one-hop neighborhood. Let users expand depth while preserving a route back to the overview.
What it enables
Finding related modules and noticing bridges across target kinds without reading every definition first.
Tradeoff to test
Distance in a force map is not a domain fact. Dense neighborhoods and unlabeled dots can conceal the reason for a connection; motion can disrupt orientation.

02 / Nx

“Why does A depend on B?”

Observed pattern
Project focus, proximity controls, expandable composite groups, path tracing, and inspection of the files that declare a dependency.
Catalog adaptation
Search for a module, focus its dependencies or reverse dependents, and inspect the exact catalog rule behind a connection. Keep paths restricted to meaningful relationship types.
What it enables
Answering a specific dependency question and sharing the relevant subgraph with another developer.
Tradeoff to test
A universal shortest path can connect unrelated semantics. A child link, conflict, and requirement must not become one undifferentiated “dependency chain.”

03 / Neo4j Bloom

“Show me just the connections I need next.”

Observed pattern
Search into a scene, inspect a node, and expand selectively by relationship type and direction. Selected nodes and parallel relationships can be grouped.
Catalog adaptation
A module action could reveal “requirements,” “implied modules,” or “used by,” with counts before expansion. Collapse a branch and undo an expansion without losing the current focus.
What it enables
Following a question step by step while controlling visual complexity.
Tradeoff to test
A partially expanded scene can look complete. Show hidden-neighbor counts and distinguish “not shown” from “does not exist.” Important actions need visible controls, not only a context menu.

Compound graphDiscover

04 / Cytoscape compound layout

“Which groups exist, and how do they connect?”

Observed pattern
Nested compound nodes keep explicit group boundaries visible while the graph retains cross-group edges.
Catalog adaptation
Use trustworthy containment, such as catalog source provenance, for groups; offer target support or capability as a selectable lens. A collapsed source could summarize its outgoing relationships.
What it enables
Understanding the shape of a composed catalog and seeing which boundaries dependencies cross.
Tradeoff to test
A module may support several target identities. Putting it in one target box can imply exclusivity; duplicating it requires a clear indication that the appearances represent the same definition.

Layered diagramUnderstand

05 / React Flow + ELK

“What follows from this module?”

Observed pattern
Named cards and routed links in a left-to-right or top-to-bottom layout. ELK supplies the automatic arrangement; React Flow supplies the interaction surface.
Catalog adaptation
Build an explanation view rooted at one module. Separate required modules, required targets, implications, and capability choices. Put the rule on the edge or in an adjacent explanation.
What it enables
Reading a small dependency chain in a deliberate order, with room for descriptions and exact target identities.
Tradeoff to test
Layering does not make this catalog a workflow. Horizontal position must not imply execution order. Child display structure and dependency structure can differ.

06 / A graph without crossing lines

“Where are relationships dense, absent, or unusual?”

Observed pattern
Rows and columns are entities; occupied cells are connections. Reordering reveals groups and bridges without drawing crossing edges.
Catalog adaptation
Try modules × target identities for support, or modules × modules for a single relationship type. Group rows by source or category; inspect a cell for the exact declaration.
What it enables
Scanning support coverage, comparing options, and spotting clusters of requirements or conflicts.
Tradeoff to test
Following a multi-hop chain is harder. Long labels need space, and an empty cell means “no declared relationship,” not “incompatible.” Conflict and co-occurrence matrices have different semantics.

07 / Hierarchical edge bundling

“Which families are coupled?”

Observed pattern
A circular hierarchy places entities around the perimeter; related links share routes through their group hierarchy.
Catalog adaptation
Group modules by source and highlight the cross-source requirements of one selected module or group. Keep exact edges available on selection.
What it enables
Recognizing patterns of coupling in a compact overview.
Tradeoff to test
Bundling can hide individual edge direction and multiplicity; perimeter labels compete for space. A grouping hierarchy must be chosen even when target support overlaps. This is a candidate overview, not an obvious default for dependency explanation.

Three exploration stories worth testing

Proposed directions to compare after we agree on the audience and task. Each could share the same catalog data and inspector, while revealing different information.

A / Explore the ecosystem

Overview → neighborhood

For: someone learning what Stack Effect can scaffold.

Begin with target kinds and compact groups. Pick a target to reveal compatible modules, choose a module, then expand only the relationships that matter.

Reference mix: Obsidian + compound groups + Bloom.

Test: Can a newcomer find a relevant module and explain one cross-target connection without getting lost?

Orient → choose → expand → inspect
B / Understand a consequence

Question → explanation

For: a developer deciding whether to use a module.

Begin with a search or “What does this need?” action. Show a labeled directed neighborhood with distinct requirements, implications, conflicts, and provider choices.

Reference mix: Nx + ELK + Bloom.

Test: Can someone explain why a module needs another target and distinguish a declaration from a resolved Blueprint?

Find → trace → explain → decide
C / Compare a catalog

Overview → evidence

For: an author or experienced user investigating catalog composition.

Begin with source groups or a support matrix. Compare two modules, isolate a relationship family, and move from an aggregate to the specific declarations.

Reference mix: matrix + Nx + bundled overview.

Test: Can an author find a support gap or an unexpected source boundary and identify its declaration?

Scope → compare → isolate → verify
Starting recommendation for discussion: compare A and B first. Keep C as an author-oriented candidate until we know whether catalog authors are a primary audience. We can evaluate all three with the same small set of catalog questions; changing the layout alone would not test the exploration story.

The graph must tell the truth

Constraints from the repository's catalog contracts and canonical domain vocabulary. These matter whichever layout we choose.

Definition and instance are different

A target kind such as package is not the same as a named TargetIdentity. Identity-specific support and dependencies need visible names or inspectable evidence.

Containment is a claim

One module definition has source provenance but can declare several support rules. Target grouping is a view of support; it must not silently imply exclusive ownership.

Support is not a requirement

supportedOn says where a module can apply. Required modules, target prerequisites, implications, children, and conflicts each communicate a different rule.

A candidate is not a selected provider

A capability requirement can have several eligible providers. A view may show candidates, but drawing all of them as required modules would invent a resolution.

A catalog is not a Blueprint

A catalog is reference data. Selection is explicit user intent. Blueprint is resolved dependency closure. An “adds these modules” interaction needs resolution and a separate, clearly named state.

Loaded and hidden are different

Filtering, collapsing, or limiting expansion must communicate what is hidden. Switching catalog sources must preserve truthful source provenance and surface loading or composition failures.

An author audit view needs an additional decision: the current loader rejects invalid catalogs before graphing. Inspecting broken references would require a separate diagnostic view rather than pretending the invalid definitions formed a loaded catalog.

The grilling session

Suggested order: settle purpose first, then graph meaning, then interactions. We should challenge each answer with a concrete task before choosing visual directions.

  1. Who must this work for first?

    A newcomer discovering features, a developer choosing modules, or an author maintaining catalogs? If we can optimize for only one, who wins?

  2. What must they accomplish in the first minute?

    Find an option, understand a dependency, compare two modules, or validate a catalog? What would count as failure even if the graph looks appealing?

  3. What is the front door?

    An entire catalog, a target picker, a module search, or a question such as “What does this require?” Should a new visitor see all 102 current prototype nodes?

  4. Which graph are we exploring?

    Definitions, support on named target identities, or a resolved Blueprint for a specific Selection? If several views exist, how does the transition make the change of meaning clear?

  5. Which relationships belong in the default view?

    Should support be conveyed through grouping rather than edges? Are prerequisites and implications always visible? Are conflicts shown only for the focused module?

  6. What should clicking a node do?

    Inspect it, refocus the graph, expand neighbors, or select it for a recipe? Which action needs a separate explicit control so exploration does not accidentally change intent?

  7. How should exploration accumulate?

    Replace the neighborhood or add to the scene? Do we need back/forward, undo expansion, pinned nodes, hidden-neighbor counts, and shareable views?

  8. What are we comparing?

    Two modules, two catalog sources, two catalog versions, or candidate capability providers? Those are distinct questions and may need different data and layouts.

  9. Where should exploration lead?

    Documentation, source declarations, a shareable explanation, or Recipe Builder with an explicit Selection? Is the explorer also a builder, or does it hand off to one?

  10. What must survive a difficult case?

    Long labels, multiple support rules, internal modules, cycles, missing providers, a large remote catalog, mobile viewing, and keyboard-only use. Which cases gate the first release?

Decision output: one primary audience, three concrete tasks, a definition of the graph's meaning, default relationship visibility, the click/expand contract, and two contrasting directions to prototype. Then test both with identical questions and catalog data.

How to compare the next prototypes

Use task completion and correct explanations as the evidence. These are proposed evaluation criteria; the references do not prove which interface will work best for our catalog.

CriterionWhat to observeWarning sign
FindabilityCan someone locate a relevant module from a task description?Random panning, repeated zooming, or reliance on exact module IDs.
ComprehensionCan they explain one relationship and its target identity?Treating a provider candidate as required, or a child link as a dependency.
OrientationCan they expand, return, and describe the current scope?Layout jumps, lost focus, or mistaking hidden nodes for missing data.
Decision supportCan they compare options and identify the next action?A visually interesting graph with no answer or useful next step.
AccessCan they reach the same information with a trackpad or keyboard?Tiny targets, hover-only evidence, or color as the only relationship cue.

Research grounding: Lee et al., Task Taxonomy for Graph Visualization (2006) motivates evaluating graph interfaces through concrete tasks. Shneiderman, The Eyes Have It (1996) supplies a useful framework for overview, filtering, details, relation, history, and extraction.
